Why invert rather than just tone it down

Softening the red and green makes the code readable but says almost nothing: a hairline meaning “22 of these lines differ somehow” is roughly the information already in the score. The page exists to answer a question the scores explicitly cannot — its own help text says they “cannot tell a shared approach from a copy”. Marking the shared runs answers it directly.

It also fixes the readability complaint as a side effect. In the renamed case the underlines sit under nearly every identifier; inverted, the renamed identifiers are the one thing left plain, so the eye lands on the skeleton that survived the renaming.

Blue, not green or red: those are spoken for by the scores and by pass/fail everywhere else in the product, and “shared” is neither good news nor bad news — it is the thing to look at.

Two shapes, two claims. A line identical end to end keeps a full-width band, indent and all: the solid run down the pane is the message. A fragment matching inside a line that differs gets a word-shaped mark that stops at the first real character — in Python the indent is shared by construction, so marking it would draw a column saying only “this code is indented” and turn the fragments into blocks. Telling the two apart at a glance is most of the value.
